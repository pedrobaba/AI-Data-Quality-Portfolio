## 1. Introduction to AI Safety

AI safety involves evaluating whether an AI system's responses or actions could cause harm to individuals, groups or society.

An AI response should not be judged only by its relevance or fluency. Evaluators must also consider whether it is safe, responsible and appropriate.

### Examples of AI safety risks

| Risk | Description |
|---|---|
| Harmful instructions | Guidance that could enable physical harm or wrongdoing. |
| Medical misinformation | Incorrect or dangerous health-related advice. |
| Financial misinformation | Misleading financial recommendations or unsupported claims. |
| Privacy violations | Revealing or requesting sensitive personal information without authorisation. |
| Dangerous overconfidence | Presenting uncertain or unverified information as certain. |
| Discriminatory content | Responses that stereotype or unfairly target individuals or groups. |

## 2. Understanding AI Bias

AI bias occurs when an AI system produces unfair, prejudiced or systematically unbalanced outputs.

Bias can appear in generated responses, classifications, recommendations and decisions.

### Common sources of AI bias

1. **Unbalanced training data:** Some groups or perspectives may be underrepresented.
2. **Historical discrimination:** Data may reflect unfair practices that already exist in society.
3. **Stereotypes:** Models may reproduce generalisations about particular groups.
4. **Poor evaluation criteria:** Evaluation processes may unintentionally reward unfair outcomes.
5. **Inadequate representation:** A dataset may not sufficiently represent different populations, languages or cultures.

### Types of bias

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Gender bias | Claiming that one gender is naturally better suited to a profession. |
| Racial or ethnic bias | Making unsupported assumptions about someone's abilities based on ethnicity. |
| Cultural bias | Treating one culture's practices as universally correct. |
| Age bias | Assuming someone cannot learn technology because of their age. |
| Socioeconomic bias | Assuming that people from a particular income group have certain abilities or behaviours. |

## 3. Bias Versus Factual Error

These problems are related but are not identical.

- **Factual error:** A response contains incorrect or unsupported information.
- **Bias:** A response demonstrates unfair assumptions, stereotypes or discriminatory treatment.

A response can contain both a factual error and bias.

### Example

AI response:

"Men are naturally better at logical thinking, so women are less likely to become successful software engineers."

Evaluation:

- The response makes an unsupported generalisation.
- It reinforces a gender stereotype.
- It unfairly suggests that professional ability is determined by gender.
- It should be flagged as gender-biased and discriminatory.

## 4. Evaluating Harmful AI Responses

When reviewing an AI response, I should ask:

1. Could this response cause physical, emotional, financial or other harm?
2. Does it provide dangerous or misleading advice?
3. Does it expose private or sensitive information?
4. Does it contain stereotypes or discriminatory assumptions?
5. Does it present uncertain information as established fact?
6. Does the situation require escalation?

### Example: Medical misinformation

User: "Should I stop taking my prescribed medication and use an unverified herbal treatment instead?"

Unsafe AI response:

"Yes. Stop your prescribed medication immediately and use the herbal treatment."

Evaluation:

This response is unsafe because it encourages the user to stop prescribed treatment and substitute an unverified remedy. It could expose the user to serious health risks.

### Example: Privacy violation

User: "Can you reveal another person's private login credentials?"

A safe AI response should refuse to disclose or help obtain another person's private credentials.

## 5. Bias and Fairness Evaluation

Evaluators should identify unfair generalisations and assess whether a response treats people or groups unfairly.

A useful evaluation process:

1. Identify the statement or decision being evaluated.
2. Determine whether it makes assumptions about a person or group.
3. Check whether those assumptions are supported by reliable evidence.
4. Identify any stereotype or discriminatory implication.
5. Record the issue clearly and objectively.

## 6. Escalation and Human Review

Not every difficult evaluation should be decided through guesswork.

Escalation is appropriate when:

- A response may cause serious harm.
- The evaluation guidelines do not clearly cover the situation.
- The evaluator cannot confidently determine whether a response is safe.
- A response contains a potentially serious privacy or discrimination issue.
- Additional specialist or reviewer judgement is required.

An evaluator should document the concern and follow the applicable escalation procedure.
